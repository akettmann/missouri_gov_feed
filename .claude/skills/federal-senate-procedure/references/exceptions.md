## 1. Unanimous consent, objections, and holds

- **What.** Nearly all Senate scheduling runs on UC. A **hold** is a senator's notice to their leader
  that they will object to UC on a measure or nomination. A hold is not in the Standing Rules. Its force
  is that overriding it costs floor time: cloture, then post-cloture hours.
- **Threshold.** One objection blocks UC.
- **Handling.**
  - The leader either negotiates a time agreement or files cloture and spends the floor time.
  - A "hotline" asks every office whether it objects to a UC request.
  - The 2011 standing order (S.Res.28, 112th) requires a senator to publish a "notice of intent to
    object" in the Congressional Record after a hold has been in place for 2 session days. Compliance
    is loose.
  - **Blanket holds** cover a whole category, such as all nominees to a department.
- **In the data.** You only see the cost: individual cloture and confirmation votes on nominees who
  once would have gone by UC or voice vote. Before the en bloc precedent, the minority used this
  systematically. That is why nomination votes are 58% of our Senate roll calls.
- **Examples.**
  - Sen. Tuberville's hold on general and flag officer promotions, Feb-Dec 2023. Hundreds of military
    promotions stalled. Most holds were released on Dec 5, 2023 *(verify count)*. During the hold,
    the Senate confirmed some chiefs individually by roll call (e.g. Gen. C.Q. Brown, Sept 2023).
  - The Senate Democratic minority's blanket objections to Trump nominees in 2025 led to the
    September 2025 en bloc precedent (§4, executive_business.md).

## 2. Filibuster and cloture

- **What.** Unlimited debate under Rule XIX. Any debatable question can be filibustered: a motion to
  proceed, a bill, an amendment, a motion to concur, a conference report, or a motion to go to
  conference. Today's filibuster is almost always a **"silent" filibuster**: the minority simply denies
  60 votes for cloture, and no one holds the floor.
- **Threshold.** 3/5 of senators duly chosen and sworn. 2/3 of those present and voting for a measure
  amending the Standing Rules. See bill_lifecycle.md for the mechanics.
- **Handling when cloture fails.**
  1. **Refile** after negotiation. Voting no and then entering a motion to reconsider preserves the
     leader's right to re-vote quickly, so leaders often vote no on their own cloture motion.
  2. **Negotiate a UC agreement**, often with 60-vote thresholds on passage and amendments.
  3. **Change the vehicle** to a fast-track statute: reconciliation, CRA, and so on.
  4. **Change the precedent** by majority (§4).
  5. Abandon the measure.
- **In the data.**
  - `On Cloture on the Motion to Proceed`: 73 votes, only 24 agreed. The minority's first block point.
  - `On the Cloture Motion` on legislation: 57 agreed and 25 rejected.
  - Lowest successful legislative cloture yea count: 60. The only exceptions are the 2025-26 en bloc
    executive resolutions.
  - Highest failed: 57 yeas.
- **Examples.**
  - 2021-22 voting rights bills (S.1, S.2093, S.2747, H.R.5746) failed cloture repeatedly.
  - S.2093 failed cloture on the motion to proceed **50-50** on 2021-06-22. The VP cannot break a
    cloture tie.
  - Fall 2025 shutdown: H.R.5371 failed cloture 13 times, then got 60-40 (see §11).
  - The "talking filibuster" proposal was rejected in January 2022 (§4).

## 3. Filling the amendment tree

- **What.** By precedent, the majority leader gets **priority recognition**. The leader can use it to
  offer every amendment the parliamentary "tree" allows (first- and second-degree, to the bill and to
  the motion to commit). No other senator can then offer an amendment without UC.
- **Handling.** The minority answers by refusing cloture ("no amendments, no cloture"). The usual
  outcome is a UC agreement that "unfills" the tree for a negotiated list of amendments, often with
  60-vote thresholds.
- **In the data.** Big bills whose amendment votes all arrive in one batch under a UC agreement.
  Bills that reach cloture and passage with **no** amendment roll calls at all.
- **Examples.** A routine tactic of both parties' leaders throughout 2015-2026 *(no single
  canonical example; check the Record for "fill the tree")*.

## 4. Points of order, appeals, and the "nuclear option"

- **What.** Any senator may raise a **point of order** that a proceeding violates a rule or
  precedent.
  - The presiding officer rules, nearly always on the **parliamentarian's** advice.
  - Some questions go straight to the Senate for decision instead of the chair, such as
    constitutional points of order and germaneness after cloture in certain cases.
  - Any senator may **appeal** the ruling. The appeal is decided by majority, and the question is
    "Shall the decision of the Chair stand as the judgment of the Senate?" If the Senate votes "Not
    Sustained", it sets a **new precedent** that binds later proceedings.
- **The "nuclear option" / "constitutional option".** Changing Senate practice by overturning the
  chair by a simple majority. A formal rules change would need 2/3 to reach cloture.
- **Handling.**
  - The majority sets up a point of order, the chair rules against it per existing precedent, and the
    majority overturns the ruling.
  - Or the majority raises a point of order to dismiss something, and the chair or the Senate sustains
    it.
  - Motions to table the appeal are common first moves.
- **In the data.**
  - `On the Decision of the Chair` (5 votes): "Not Sustained" means precedent was changed.
  - `On the Point of Order` (7 votes).
  - `On the Motion to Table` (an appeal or point of order).
  - `On the Motion to Reconsider` right after a failed cloture vote: the setup for the 2025 en bloc
    precedent.
- **Precedents and attempts, 2013-2026.**

## 5. Budget reconciliation and the Byrd rule

- **What.** Congressional Budget Act §310 (2 U.S.C. 641).
  - A **budget resolution** (concurrent resolution) includes *reconciliation instructions* telling
    committees to change spending, revenue, or the debt limit by set amounts.
  - The resulting reconciliation bill gets fast-track protection in the Senate. The motion to proceed
    is non-debatable, debate is capped at **20 hours** (10 for a conference report), and only germane
    amendments are allowed. **No filibuster, so a simple majority passes it.**
  - By practice, one reconciliation bill per subject (spending, revenue, debt limit) per budget
    resolution. Revising a budget resolution under §304 can make another one possible (used in 2021).
- **Byrd rule (§313, 2 U.S.C. 644).** In the Senate, "extraneous" provisions are out of order. The
  tests:
  - no budgetary effect;
  - budgetary effect merely incidental to the policy;
  - increases the deficit beyond the budget window;
  - outside the reporting committee's jurisdiction;
  - fails to meet the instructions;
  - changes Social Security.
- **Handling.**
  1. Before the floor, the parliamentarian holds the **"Byrd bath"**: each side argues provisions,
     and the parliamentarian advises which ones would fall. The majority usually strips or rewrites
     those provisions rather than face a point of order.
  2. On the floor, a Byrd point of order strikes the provision unless **3/5 of senators sworn** vote
     to waive it.
  3. When debate time expires, pending and queued amendments get votes with essentially no debate:
     the **vote-a-rama**.
  4. Budget resolutions themselves get 50 hours of debate and their own vote-a-rama.
- **In the data.**
  - The busiest Senate days in our cache are all vote-a-ramas:
    - 2022-08-07 IRA (`HR5376`), 37 roll calls;
    - 2021-03-06 ARP (`HR1319`), 34;
    - 2021-08-10, 28: IIJA passage (`HR3684`, a bipartisan bill under cloture) followed by the FY2022 budget resolution (S.Con.Res.14) vote-a-rama;
    - 2025-06-30 OBBBA (`HR1`), 24, plus 19 on 2025-07-01;
    - 2021-02-04/05 FY2021 budget resolution (`SCONRES5`), 19 + 22.
  - Look for `On the Motion` rows that are motions to waive (3/5) and `On the Motion to Recommit`
    (motion to commit with instructions, 7 votes, all rejected).
- **Examples.**
  - 2017 "skinny repeal" of the ACA failed 49-51 (July 28, 2017).
  - TCJA (P.L. 115-97) passed under reconciliation in Dec 2017. The Byrd rule forced the individual
    tax cuts to expire after 10 years.
  - ARP (P.L. 117-2), Mar 2021. The parliamentarian advised that the $15 minimum wage was extraneous
    (Feb 25, 2021), and it was dropped.
  - IRA (P.L. 117-169), Aug 2022, 51-50 with the VP's tie-break.
  - FY2025 budget resolution (H.Con.Res.14), leading to OBBBA (H.R.1, P.L. 119-21). It passed the
    Senate 51-50 with VP Vance breaking the tie on 2025-07-01 and was signed July 4, 2025. It raised
    the debt limit by $5 trillion *(verify figure)*.
  - The FY2026 budget resolution (`SCONRES33`) was agreed 50-48 on 2026-04-23. A budget resolution is
    usually the first step toward another reconciliation bill; check its instructions before saying so.

## 6. Budget Act points of order and waivers

- **What.** Points of order that enforce budget totals:
  - §302(f): exceeds a committee allocation.
  - §311: breaches the aggregates.
  - §425: unfunded mandates.
  - Senate PAYGO (from the budget resolution).
  - Emergency-designation points of order.
  - The §306 jurisdiction rule.
- **Threshold.** A sustained point of order kills the bill or amendment. A **motion to waive** needs
  3/5 of senators sworn (§904).
- **Handling.** The bill manager moves to waive "all applicable sections". Most big bipartisan
  spending bills get a waiver vote.
- **In the data.** These usually appear as `On the Motion` with `dtl_desc` / `vote_desc` mentioning a
  "motion to waive". They sit right before an amendment or passage vote and are decided at 60.

## 7. Congressional Review Act (CRA)

- **What.** 5 U.S.C. 801-808. Lets Congress disapprove an agency rule by joint resolution.
  - **Senate fast track.** If the committee does not report within 20 calendar days, a petition signed
    by **30 senators** discharges it. The motion to proceed is non-debatable, debate is capped at 10
    hours, and **no amendments** are allowed. A simple majority passes it.
  - Window: the Senate action period is 60 session days after the rule is received.
  - **Lookback.** Rules submitted within the last 60 legislative or session days of the prior session
    get a fresh window in the new session. This is why CRA waves follow a party change in the White
    House.
  - Once a rule is disapproved, the agency cannot reissue a "substantially the same" rule without new
    statutory authority.
- **Handling of disputes.** Whether something is a "rule" is decided case by case. GAO opinions and
  parliamentarian advice matter, and the 2025 California waiver precedent (§4) shows the majority can
  override them. A late filing gets a point of order and loses its fast-track status.
- **In the data.**
  - `vote_desc` contains "chapter 8 of title 5".
  - Senate passage votes in our cache:
    - 117th: 7 passed, 2 defeated;
    - 118th: 13 passed, 4 defeated (a divided Congress; most were vetoed by Biden);
    - 119th: 24 passed, 1 defeated, through Oct 2026.
  - Also count `Bill Passed` rows where a House-numbered measure is used.
- **Examples.**
  - 2017-18: 16 CRA disapprovals enacted, after only one ever before (2001).
  - 2021: 3 enacted, including methane.
  - 2023-24: Biden vetoed every CRA disapproval that reached him, and the Senate override attempts
    failed. See the 5 `Veto Sustained` rows: SJRES11 (50-50), SJRES24, SJRES9, SJRES32, SJRES38.
  - 2025: a large wave including the California waivers (H.J.Res.87-89) and CFPB rules.

## 8. Other fast-track statutes

All of these get a privileged or non-debatable route to the floor and pass by simple majority, so
filibusters do not apply. The usual majority defense is a **point of order that the measure does not
qualify**, or a **motion to table**. The House-side defense is different: special rules that "turn off
the clock" (see the federal-house-procedure skill).

## 9. Discharging a committee

- **What.** In the Senate, a regular motion to discharge is debatable, so it practically needs
  cloture, and committees are rarely bypassed this way. Rule XIV (bill_lifecycle.md) is the normal
  bypass. **Statutory discharges** (CRA, NEA, WPR, AECA, ICA, 502B) are the real-world uses.
- **Power-sharing discharge (117th).** Under S.Res.27 (2021), committees were split evenly. If a
  committee tied on a bill or nomination, the majority **or minority** leader could move to discharge
  it, with 4 hours of debate and a majority vote.
- **In the data.**
  - `On the Motion to Discharge` with `PN`: 24 agreed, all between 2021-03-11 and 2022-08-06. These
    are the power-sharing discharges of tied committee votes on nominations.
  - With `SJRES`: mostly AECA and CRA, 29 rejected and 2 agreed.
  - With `HR4` in 2025: the ICA rescissions discharge.

## 10. Vetoes and overrides

- **Handling.** The President returns the bill to the originating chamber with a veto message. The
  chamber may vote to override (2/3 of those present and voting), refer the message to committee,
  table it, or ignore it. Most vetoes get no override vote, or only a failed one. If the originating
  chamber fails, the other never votes.
- **In the data.** `On Overriding the Veto`: 5 rows in our cache, all `Veto Sustained`, all Biden
  vetoes of CRA resolutions (2023-24).
- **2015-2026.**
  - Obama: 12 vetoes total. The only override was **JASTA** on 2016-09-28 (Senate 97-1).
  - Trump (first term): 10 vetoes, 1 override, the **FY2021 NDAA** on 2021-01-01 (Senate 81-13). Most
    other vetoes were of fast-track disapprovals (border emergency, Yemen, Iran, Saudi arms).
  - Biden: 13 vetoes, 0 overrides *(verify count)*.
  - Trump (second term): check the Senate's "Vetoes" page for any vetoes to date. Do not assume none.

## 11. Appropriations lapses, CRs, and shutdowns

- **What.** If the regular appropriations acts are not enacted by Oct 1, Congress passes a
  **continuing resolution** (CR) or an omnibus or minibus. Funding lapses when neither is in place. In
  the Senate, a CR or omnibus needs 60 for cloture, so the minority holds a veto.
- **Handling.**
  - Repeated cloture votes with no agreement. Negotiators trade side-commitments, such as a promised
    vote on a related issue.
  - The final package often rides on a **House-passed shell** (H.R. number) amended by the Senate,
    then sent back to the House.
  - Leaders also use UC time agreements to set amendment votes.
- **In the data.** A run of `Cloture … Rejected` rows on the same `HR` bill. Fall 2025: H.R.5371
  failed 13 cloture votes from 2025-10-01 to 2025-11-04 (49-55 yeas). It got 60-40 on 2025-11-09
  (motion to proceed) and 2025-11-10 (bill).
- **Lapses 2015-2026.**
  - 2018-01-20 to 01-22 (3 days).
  - 2018-02-09 (hours).
  - 2018-12-22 to 2019-01-25 (35 days, partial).
  - **2025-10-01 to 2025-11-12 (43 days, the longest).**
  - 2026: a partial lapse 2026-01-31 to 02-03, then a DHS-only lapse starting 2026-02-14 *(end date
    disputed between sources, verify)*.

## 12. Debt limit

- **What.** A statutory borrowing ceiling. It is raised or **suspended** by law, which in the Senate
  normally needs 60 for cloture. Reconciliation can also change it (§5).
- **Handling exceptions.**
  - **2021.** In Oct 2021, a $480 billion short-term increase got 61 for cloture (P.L. 117-50).
  - **Dec 2021 one-time carve-out.** S.610 (P.L. 117-71) created a one-time, non-filibusterable
    procedure for a single debt-limit joint resolution. Cloture on S.610 itself got 64. The
    $2.5 trillion increase (S.J.Res.33, P.L. 117-73) then passed 50-49. *(verify P.L. numbers)*
  - **Fiscal Responsibility Act** (P.L. 118-5, June 2023): suspension to Jan 2025, passed the Senate
    63-36.
  - **OBBBA** (2025) raised the limit inside reconciliation.
- **Earlier window.** The 2015 (BBA 2015), 2018 (BBA 2018) and 2019 (BBA 2019) suspensions were
  enacted as part of bipartisan budget deals.

## 13. Tie votes and the Vice President

- **What.** Art. I §3: the VP votes only to break a tie. A tie on a majority question fails unless
  the VP votes yea. The VP cannot vote on legislative cloture: 3/5 counts sworn senators, and a tie is
  well short of that. The VP can break ties on nomination cloture, which is a majority question.
- **Handling.** The leader schedules the VP's presence for expected ties. A missing senator can turn
  a sure 51-49 into a 50-49 or a tie.
- **In the data.** Rows with `yea_count = nay_count` and a positive result: confirmations,
  nomination cloture, IRA passage, OBBBA passage, the rescissions discharge, the 2021 budget
  resolution. Voteview's totals exclude the VP. Kamala Harris cast a record number of tie-breaking
  votes as VP (2021-2025). JD Vance broke ties on Hegseth's confirmation (2025-01-24, PN117) and OBBBA.

## 14. Evenly divided Senate (117th power-sharing)

- **What.** 50-50 in 2021-Jan 2023 with VP Harris giving Democrats control. S.Res.27 (Feb 2021) set
  the rules:
  - equal committee membership;
  - equal budgets;
  - the majority keeps the chairs;
  - a discharge motion for tied committee votes (§9).
- **Handling.** Expect tied committee votes, followed by floor discharge motions and many 50-50 floor
  ties.
- After Jan 2023 (118th), Democrats held 51 and normal committee ratios returned.

## 15. Quorum, attendance, and adjournment fights

- **What.** A quorum is a majority of senators. A "live" quorum call or a `Motion for Attendance`
  (instructing the Sergeant at Arms to request or compel absent senators' attendance) is used to force
  attendance, often during overnight sessions. A `Motion to Adjourn` is a minority dilatory tool, or a
  majority tool to end the day.
- **In the data.** `On the Motion for Attendance` (3, agreed); `On the Motion to Adjourn` (7, 1
  agreed). These mark late-night or weekend sessions, e.g. reconciliation and the 2025 shutdown.

## 16. Rule XIX and other discipline

- **What.** Rule XIX bars imputing unworthy conduct to another senator. If a senator is called to
  order and the Senate agrees, that senator cannot speak further on the question without leave.
- **Example.** Sen. Warren was silenced on 2017-02-07 during the Sessions nomination debate. The
  ruling was upheld on appeal 49-43 *(verify tally)*.
- Censure and expulsion are rare: no expulsion in the window. Sen. Menendez resigned in Aug 2024 after
  his conviction, before any expulsion vote.

## 17. Electoral vote count objections

- **What.** In a joint session, an objection to a state's electoral votes splits the chambers to
  vote separately. The Senate vote is `On the Objection`.
- **Thresholds.**
  - Before 2022, an objection needed 1 senator and 1 representative.
  - The **Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022** raised that to 1/5 of each chamber and narrowed the
    grounds.
- **In the data.** 2021-01-06: objections to Arizona (6-93) and Pennsylvania (7-92) were "Not
  Sustained". There were no Senate objection votes in 2025.

## 18. Workarounds: shell bills and blue slips

- **Shell bills.** The Origination Clause (Art. I §7) requires revenue bills to start in the House,
  and custom extends that to appropriations. The Senate therefore takes up an unrelated House-passed
  `HR` bill and replaces its text with a substitute. Examples:
  - IIJA rode on H.R.3684.
  - The CHIPS Act rode on H.R.4346.
  - H.R.2882 (a Udall trust bill) was the vehicle for the March 2024 appropriations package, with a
    run of tabling votes on 2024-03-23.

  Never infer a bill's content from its original title alone. Read the `vote_desc` / `dtl_desc` of
  the substitute.
- **Blue slips.** The Judiciary Committee chair asks home-state senators to return a blue slip on
  judicial and U.S. attorney nominees. The practice is the chair's policy, not a rule.
  - Since 2017-18 (Chairman Grassley), a missing blue slip no longer blocks **circuit** nominees.
  - It still blocks district judges and U.S. attorneys, a point of public dispute in 2025.
  - Handling depends on the chair; check the chair's stated policy for that Congress.
