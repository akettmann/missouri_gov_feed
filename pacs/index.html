How PACs move money

TL;DR: A PAC is a group that collects money and spends it on elections. The law lets PACs give money to other PACs, and some groups that give to PACs don't have to say where their own money came from. So money can be passed from group to group before it reaches a candidate, and the public record shows only the last group, not who really paid. That's a legal loophole for hiding who gave money to whom, and both parties use it.

Full Description

Political action committees (PACs) pool money and spend it on elections. Most of that is ordinary and fully reported. But because a PAC can give to another PAC, and because some groups that give to PACs don't have to name their own donors, money can pass through several hands before it reaches a candidate, and the report the public sees names only the last one. This page explains how that works in Missouri and federal races and how to follow the money yourself. The practices described here are used by groups across the political spectrum; examples are drawn from reporting on both parties.

What a PAC is

In Missouri races

Missouri law sorts political money into committees, each registered with the Missouri Ethics Commission (MEC):

  • Candidate committees raise and spend for one candidate. They can't give to other candidate committees (including federal ones) or to PACs.1
  • Continuing committees, also called political action committees: the constitution uses both names, and the MEC has said they're the same thing.1 A PAC can give to candidates (within the limits below), to parties and to other PACs.
  • Political party committees: state, county and district committees of a party, with their own limits.1

Corporations and labor unions can't give to candidate committees or parties directly, but they can set up a PAC funded by their members, officers, employees or shareholders, and PACs may take money from corporations, associations and partnerships.1

In federal races

The Federal Election Commission (FEC) recognizes several kinds of PAC:

  • Separate segregated fund (SSF): a corporate or union PAC with a sponsoring organization. It may ask only certain people tied to that organization (members, stockholders, certain employees and their families) for money.11
  • Nonconnected PAC: no sponsoring corporation or union; it can ask the general public for money.11
  • Leadership PAC: a committee "directly or indirectly established, financed, maintained or controlled by a candidate" that is not the candidate's own campaign committee.12
  • Super PAC (independent expenditure-only committee): may take unlimited amounts from individuals, corporations, unions and other PACs, but may not give to candidates or coordinate with them.12 Super PACs follow from two 2010 rulings: Citizens United v. FEC held that corporations can't be barred from independent spending,14 and SpeechNow.org v. FEC then struck down limits on contributions to groups that only make independent expenditures.15
  • Hybrid PAC: keeps a limited account for giving to candidates and a separate unlimited account for independent spending.12

What PACs are for

Most PAC activity is what the law intends:

  • Pooling small gifts. Members of a union, trade group or company can each give a little and have it spent together.
  • A lawful channel for organizations. Corporations and unions that can't give to candidates directly can take part through a PAC their people fund.1
  • Supporting allies. In the FEC's description, leadership PAC money is "given to other Federal candidates," pays for the officeholder's travel when campaigning for them, or goes to party committees and state and local candidates.13
  • Independent advocacy. Groups spend on ads for or against candidates and ballot measures without coordinating with a campaign.

Every PAC reports the donors who give to it. The problem this page is about is narrower: when a donor is itself a PAC or a nonprofit, the trail continues somewhere else, and sometimes it ends.

How money can be layered

None of the patterns below is illegal on its own, and each has ordinary uses. Together they can make it hard or impossible for a voter to see where money started.19 What the law does forbid is disguising the true donor: giving in another person's name.7,17

One source, many PACs

Missouri's limits apply per donor, and a PAC is a donor. A group that funds several PACs can have each one give the maximum to the same candidate. The Missouri Independent reported in 2024 that the Missouri Health Care Association stopped funding its own PAC in 2017 and began giving to two new ones, DSV PAC and RQC PAC, which share a treasurer; recipients include Senate Minority Leader Doug Beck (D). Six PACs run by Strategic Capitol Consulting each gave $2,400 to Brad Hudson (R) on the same day, $14,400 in all; and the Missouri Bankers Association runs a statewide PAC plus seven regional ones.19 The candidates report each check as coming from the PAC that wrote it.

PAC-to-PAC chains

Since the courts struck down Missouri's PAC-to-PAC ban,5 money can move through a chain of committees. Each committee reports only who gave to it, so a candidate's report shows the last PAC in the chain. Finding the original donors means opening that PAC's own reports, then the reports of each committee that funded it, and so on.

Nonprofits that don't name their donors

Tax-exempt nonprofits such as 501(c)(4) "social welfare" groups and 501(c)(6) trade associations are generally not required to disclose their contributors publicly.18 When such a group gives to a PAC or ballot committee, the PAC reports the nonprofit as the donor and the trail stops there. This is often called "dark money." Two Missouri examples from 2024, one on each side: the Concord Fund, a 501(c)(4),21 was the largest donor to candidate PACs, giving $6 million in the effort to nominate Will Scharf (R) for attorney general;19 and the Sixteen Thirty Fund, also a 501(c)(4),22 gave $4.5 million to the campaign for the abortion-rights amendment, whose largest donors were "four out-of-state progressive nonprofits that are not required to disclose their donors."20

Names that don't say who's behind them

A PAC's name doesn't have to identify its funders. In the 2024 reporting, money from J&J Ventures reached candidates through PACs named Another Viceroy PAC, Paladin PAC and Rowdy PAC, and eight candidates got amounts that would have exceeded the limit from a single PAC.19 Names like these also defeat keyword matching, including this site's (see below).

Timing

Missouri's last report before an election is due eight days before it and covers money through the twelfth day before; the next one is due 30 days after.9 Gifts made in that gap appear only after the vote, unless a single contribution exceeds $5,000 and triggers a 48-hour report.8 Federal campaigns have a similar 48-hour rule for gifts of $1,000 or more late in a race.16

Trace it yourself

  1. Start from a candidate's donors (on this site, the Money in section of any official's profile) and note donors that are committees, PACs or organizations rather than people.
  2. Look the committee up in the MEC's campaign finance search by name or MEC ID, and open its disclosure reports to see who gave to it. The MEC also has a search for single contributions over $5,000.23
  3. Repeat for each committee among that PAC's donors until you reach people or companies.
  4. For federal races, the FEC's data pages list each committee's receipts by contributor, including transfers from other committees.24
  5. If the trail reaches a nonprofit, public records usually stop: its tax return shows amounts but not, in general, donor names.18
  6. FollowTheMoney.org collects state campaign finance across years and states, which helps with donors who give in many places.25

How this site shows it

  • Each profile's Money in section lists donors as the official's committee reported them: totals by cycle, top donors, top employers and gift sizes. A PAC shows up under its own name; the site does not yet follow PAC-to-PAC chains back to the original donors.
  • The awards rank money from PACs, committees and parties, counted together because the MEC files PACs as committees (methodology).
  • Interest-and-votes views assign donors to industries by keywords in their names. Opaque PAC names, leadership PACs and pass-through PACs usually match no industry, so those views undercount (methodology).

As everywhere on this site, a donation shown next to a vote is co-occurrence, not evidence the vote was bought.
